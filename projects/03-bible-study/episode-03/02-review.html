Episode 3 — Review Questions Discussion

Jesus Christ, Divine Bridegroom
Scripture Focus: John 3–5

Question 1

In John 3:1-2, Nicodemus comes to Jesus by night. What does this action symbolize?

  • a. A lack of knowledge
  • b. The light of the Holy Spirit
  • c. Spiritual darkness
  • d. Both A & C

Answer: D — Both A & C

Discussion

To understand why Nicodemus coming "by night" carries such weight, we must first appreciate the literary and theological architecture of John's Gospel. From the very opening of the Prologue, St. John establishes a governing cosmic opposition: light and darkness.

"The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it." (John 1:5)

This is not merely poetic atmosphere. For John, light and darkness are moral and spiritual categories. Light is identified with Christ Himself — "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12) — and with the illuminating grace of the Holy Spirit. Darkness, by contrast, is the condition of the soul that has not yet received that illumination. It is the natural habitat of sin, ignorance, and unbelief.

When John tells us that Nicodemus came to Jesus by night, he is doing something the Church Fathers recognized immediately: he is placing Nicodemus squarely within the realm of darkness — not as a moral condemnation, but as a theological statement about his current spiritual condition.

Darkness as Spiritual Blindness (option C)

Nicodemus is a Pharisee and "a ruler of the Jews" — a man of considerable religious standing. He even acknowledges that Jesus comes from God ("no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him", John 3:2). And yet, for all his religious learning, he remains in the dark. He has not yet come to faith. He approaches Jesus secretly, under cover of night, because the full light of revelation has not yet pierced his heart.

St. Augustine, commenting on this passage, notes that Nicodemus represents those souls who possess a kind of preliminary faith — they see the signs, they sense the divine presence — but have not yet made the full passage from darkness to light. He writes that Nicodemus "came to Jesus, but came by night; that is, he came to the Light, but came in darkness." The darkness is not merely external. It is the condition of his interior life at that moment.

Darkness as Lack of Understanding (option A)

This same darkness is expressed immediately in what follows. When Jesus tells Nicodemus that "unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3), Nicodemus responds with a question that reveals how deeply carnal — how earthbound — his thinking still is: "How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb and be born?" (John 3:4).

He hears the words of eternal life and interprets them biologically. He stands before the Word made flesh and cannot yet read the language of the Spirit. This is precisely the lack of knowledge that the darkness symbolizes. His mind is not yet illumined by grace, and so the spiritual realities Jesus speaks of remain opaque to him.

St. Cyril of Alexandria draws the same connection: Nicodemus's inability to understand spiritual rebirth flows from the very darkness he carries into the encounter. Knowledge and light, in Johannine theology, are inseparable. To be in darkness is to be without true understanding.

Why not option B?

Option B — "the light of the Holy Spirit" — is precisely the opposite of what the night represents. The Holy Spirit is the agent of illumination, the one who leads us into all truth (John 16:13). To come by night is to come before that illumination has taken hold. It is the absence of the Holy Spirit's light, not its presence.

The Catechism and Baptismal Typology

It is worth noting that the Church has always read this passage in close connection with the Sacrament of Baptism. The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that Baptism is the sacrament of illumination (CCC 1216), drawing directly on the early Christian title for Baptism: photismos — enlightenment. To be baptized is to pass from darkness into the marvelous light of Christ (1 Peter 2:9).

Nicodemus's night visit, then, is not merely a narrative detail. It is a theological portrait of the soul before Baptism — dwelling in spiritual darkness and ignorance, yet drawn nonetheless toward the Light. Jesus does not send him away. He meets him in the darkness and speaks to him of water and the Spirit (John 3:5). The very conversation foreshadows what Baptism will accomplish: the passage from night into light, from ignorance into knowledge, from death into new life.

Question 2

True or False: The Samaritan woman's adultery is symbolic of the idolatrous history of the Northern tribes.

Answer: TRUE

Discussion

At first reading, the encounter at Jacob's Well in John 4 appears to be a straightforward story of personal conversion: a woman with a troubled past meets Jesus, comes to recognize Him as the Messiah, and runs to tell her village. And it is certainly that. But the Catholic tradition, following the method of the Church Fathers and rooted in the deep logic of salvation history, recognizes that John has embedded a far richer meaning in the detail that the woman has had five husbands — and that the man she currently lives with is not her husband.

The Historical Key: 2 Kings 17

To grasp the symbolism, we must go back to one of the most consequential events in Israel's history: the fall of the Northern Kingdom of Samaria to the Assyrian Empire in 722 BC. The Assyrian policy was to displace conquered populations. When they deported the Israelites of the North, they repopulated the land with five foreign nations:

"And the king of Assyria brought people from Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim, and placed them in the cities of Samaria instead of the people of Israel." (2 Kings 17:24)

Each of these five nations brought its own gods. The text goes on to describe the syncretistic religion that resulted — a mixture of the worship of Yahweh with the worship of the gods of these foreign peoples (2 Kings 17:29-41). The Samaritans thus became a people who, as the text says, "feared the Lord but also served their own gods."

Five nations. Five foreign gods. Five husbands.

The Prophetic Background: Israel as Unfaithful Bride

This identification is not an interpretive novelty. The entire prophetic tradition of the Old Testament consistently employs the marriage metaphor to describe the covenant between God and Israel — and Israel's idolatry as adultery against the divine Bridegroom.

The prophet Hosea is commanded by God to marry a faithless woman as a living parable of this reality (Hosea 1–3). Ezekiel gives an even more extended and devastating account of Israel's spiritual adultery in chapters 16 and 23, describing how Jerusalem and Samaria played the harlot with the nations around them. Jeremiah uses the same language (Jeremiah 3). In every case, the idolatry of the Northern tribes — their abandonment of the one true God for the gods of the nations — is spoken of as marital unfaithfulness.

When Jesus speaks to the Samaritan woman and reveals that she has had five husbands, He is not merely exposing her personal sin. He is speaking the language of the prophets to the daughter of the Northern Kingdom. You have had five husbands — five false gods, five nations, five objects of your misplaced worship. And the one you have now is not your husband — the current spiritual disorder, the ongoing syncretic half-worship that has marked Samaria for centuries, is no true covenant at all.

Jesus as the True Bridegroom of Israel

This is why the scene takes place at Jacob's Well. In the Old Testament, wells are betrothal sites. Abraham's servant finds a wife for Isaac at a well. Jacob meets Rachel at a well. Moses meets Zipporah at a well. The pattern is unmistakable: a man meets a woman at a well, and a marriage covenant follows. John's first readers would have recognized this pattern immediately.

Jesus comes to Jacob's Well not as a passing traveler but as the Divine Bridegroom of Israel, come to reclaim His unfaithful Bride. He offers her not ordinary water but living water — a spousal gift, the gift of Himself, the gift of the Holy Spirit (John 7:37-39). He reveals that He knows her history — all five husbands — not to shame her, but to show her that He knows her fully and loves her still.

This is the deepest logic of the Incarnation itself, as the Catechism teaches: "The entire Christian life bears the mark of the spousal love of Christ and the Church" (CCC 1617). The New Covenant is a New Marriage. And Jesus comes to Samaria — to the very heartland of Israel's ancient faithlessness — to offer that covenant to a woman who embodies the whole history of her people's wandering.

The Samaritan Woman as Icon of the Church

The Church Fathers, particularly Origen and St. Augustine, read the Samaritan woman as a type of the Church drawn from the Gentile nations — longing, without knowing it, for the true Bridegroom. Her words "I know that Messiah is coming" (John 4:25) reveal a deeper hunger beneath all her restless searching. She has been seeking the wrong husbands all along. In Christ, she finds the One for whom her heart was made.

This is the Catholic reading of Scripture at its most beautiful: nothing is wasted, nothing is merely incidental. The five husbands of one woman at a well in Samaria carry the weight of centuries of salvation history — and point forward to the mercy of the Divine Bridegroom who came to make all things new.
