Episode 3 — Reflection Questions Discussion

Jesus Christ, Divine Bridegroom
Scripture Focus: John 3–5

Question 1

Every year on the Third Sunday in Lent, the Church invites the faithful to reflect on the story of the woman at the well. In light of what Dr. Bergsma shared in this episode, why do you think the Church returns to this passage year after year? What aspects of the Samaritan woman's story reflect the themes of the Lenten season?

Discussion

The Church is never arbitrary in her liturgical choices. When she returns to a text year after year — and she has done so for centuries — it is because she has discerned in that text an inexhaustible depth of spiritual nourishment suited to a particular moment in the sacred year. The story of the Samaritan woman at Jacob's Well (John 4:1–42) is one of the most theologically dense passages in all of Sacred Scripture, and its placement on the Third Sunday of Lent is an act of profound pastoral wisdom.

The Lenten Context: Preparation for Baptism

To understand why the Church chooses this passage for Lent, we must first recall what Lent actually is. It is not, at its origins, merely a season of personal penance for the already baptized. Its ancient heart is catechumenal — it is the final and most intense stage of preparation for those who will receive Baptism at the Easter Vigil. The Third, Fourth, and Fifth Sundays of Lent in Year A of the lectionary correspond to the three great scrutinies — solemn rites of purification and enlightenment celebrated with the elect, those preparing to enter the Church.

The scrutinies are precisely what the name suggests: an honest examination, before God and the community, of the condition of the soul. They are a laying bare of interior darkness so that it might be driven out and replaced with the light of Christ. And there is no better icon of this movement in all of Scripture than the Samaritan woman.

She comes to the well alone, at noon — the sixth hour, when the sun is highest and shadows are shortest. Some commentators have seen in this detail the opposite of Nicodemus: she comes in full light, but her life is one of hidden darkness. She has been drawing water alone, likely because the shame of her history has made her an outcast from the company of the other women who would come at dawn. She is isolated by sin, just as every soul preparing for Baptism must come to recognize their isolation from God through the weight of sin.

The Dynamics of Conversion

What unfolds at the well is a masterclass in the dynamics of conversion — which is why the Church, in her wisdom, returns to it every year. Jesus does not begin with accusation. He begins with thirst: "Give me a drink" (John 4:7). He enters the woman's world, meets her where she is, and draws her into conversation. This is the pattern of divine grace: it does not overwhelm but invites, not condemns but reveals.

As the conversation deepens, Jesus gently brings her to the point of self-knowledge. "Go, call your husband" (John 4:16). He already knows the answer. He is not gathering information — He is inviting her to honesty, to the same interior examination that the scrutinies demand of the elect. And she responds, to her credit, with a kind of courageous half-truth: "I have no husband." Jesus honors the truth in her words and fills in what she left unspoken.

This movement — from evasion to acknowledgment, from the surface to the depths — is the essential movement of Lent. It is the movement of metanoia, the Greek word the Church uses for repentance, meaning not merely feeling sorry but a turning of the mind, a reorientation of the whole person toward God.

Thirst as the Lenten Condition

At the deepest level, the story of the Samaritan woman is a story about thirst. She comes to the well because she is physically thirsty. But Jesus diagnoses a deeper thirst beneath the surface — the thirst of a soul that has been seeking in all the wrong places the one thing that can satisfy: living communion with the living God.

"Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again, but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again." (John 4:13–14)

This is one of the most penetrating lines in the Gospel. It is a description of every human life lived without God. We keep returning to the same wells — pleasure, approval, comfort, distraction — and they keep running dry. The woman has had five husbands. She has tried again and again to fill the aching void at the center of her life. And Jesus meets her in that exhaustion with an offer that changes everything.

Lent is the season when the Church asks us to sit with our thirst. To stop filling ourselves with things that do not satisfy and allow ourselves to feel, honestly, how deep the longing goes. The Samaritan woman is our companion in this. Her transformation — from isolated, shame-ridden outcast to the first evangelist of her village — is the promise of what Lent, lived well, can accomplish in each of us.

Baptismal Typology

Finally, we cannot miss the sacramental dimension. The Catechism of the Catholic Church identifies water, from the very first pages of Genesis, as a primary symbol of the Holy Spirit's action (CCC 694). The living water Jesus offers the Samaritan woman is, as He Himself will make explicit in John 7:38–39, the Holy Spirit. And the Holy Spirit is given preeminently in Baptism.

The Church places this passage before her catechumens in the final weeks before their Baptism because it is a portrait of what is about to happen to them. They, like the woman, are about to receive living water. They, like her, will be known completely by the One who gives it — and loved completely in that knowledge. And they, like her, will be sent to proclaim what they have received.

Question 2

What are your thoughts on the connection between the "living water" Jesus offers the Samaritan woman and the description of Solomon's bride as a "well of living water" in Song of Songs 4:15?

Discussion

The connection between John 4 and Song of Songs 4:15 is one of the most beautiful instances of typological fulfillment in all of Scripture, and it has not been lost on the great tradition of Catholic biblical interpretation.

The Text of Song of Songs

In Song of Songs 4:15, the bridegroom Solomon addresses his bride with an extraordinary image:

"A garden fountain, a well of living water, and flowing streams from Lebanon."

The bride herself is described as a source of living water. She is the well from which the bridegroom draws life and refreshment. The entire section (4:12–5:1) describes the bride as a locked garden, a sealed fountain — an image of purity, abundance, and the intimate exclusivity of the covenant bond. She is, for her bridegroom, the source of everything he needs.

The Church, following the great Origen in his Commentary on the Song of Songs, has always read this text on multiple levels simultaneously. At the literal level, it is a love poem celebrating human marriage. But at the deeper allegorical level — and the Fathers are unanimous that this deeper reading is the text's primary spiritual meaning — the bridegroom is God, and the bride is Israel and, by extension, the Church and the individual soul.

The Inversion and Its Meaning

Now consider what happens in John 4. Jesus, the Divine Bridegroom, comes to the Samaritan woman — a daughter of the lost Northern Kingdom, a figure of Israel's scattered and wayward Bride — at a well. And He does not draw water from her. Instead, He offers her living water from Himself.

Here is the profound inversion that the typology makes visible: in Song of Songs, the bride is the well of living water for the bridegroom. In John 4, the Bridegroom offers living water to a bride who has run completely dry.

This is not a contradiction but a fulfillment. It shows us the precise nature of what sin has done to Israel — and to every human soul. The bride who was meant, in God's original design, to be a garden fountain, a well of living water, has become instead a woman returning daily to an earthly well, drawing water that cannot satisfy. The image of the Song has been shattered by the history of human faithlessness.

And so the Divine Bridegroom comes — not to condemn the broken image, but to restore it. He offers her the living water that will become in her "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14). He is not taking from her. He is giving her back what she was always meant to be.

The Holy Spirit as Living Water

Jesus identifies this living water explicitly with the Holy Spirit in John 7:38–39:

"Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.' Now this he said about the Spirit."

The Holy Spirit is the living water of the New Covenant. And the reception of the Holy Spirit — particularly in Baptism and Confirmation — restores to the soul what sin had taken away: the capacity to be, in the language of Song of Songs, a garden fountain, a source of living water for others.

This is what the Samaritan woman becomes. She receives the living water from Jesus. She returns to her village. And from her flows the evangelistic proclamation that brings her whole community to Christ. She is transformed, by encounter with the Bridegroom, from a dry and isolated woman into the very thing Solomon celebrated in his bride: a well of living water for those around her.

The Cross as the Deepest Fulfillment

We cannot close this reflection without noting that the ultimate source of this living water is the pierced side of Christ on Calvary. John tells us with deliberate solemnity: "one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out" (John 19:34). The Church Fathers — Augustine most memorably — saw in this the birth of the Church from the side of the New Adam, just as Eve was drawn from the side of the first Adam. The living water that flows from Christ's wounded side is the water of Baptism, and the blood is the Eucharist.

The Bridegroom gives Himself entirely for His Bride. He becomes, on the Cross, the inexhaustible well of living water that His Bride was always meant to be.

Question 3

Dr. Bergsma described the man Jesus healed in John 5 as an "icon of the people of Israel." When you examine this passage, what dimensions of the paralyzed man's story reflect the history of the Israelites in the Old Testament? What aspects of the man's encounter with Our Lord can offer insight into the Christian life today?

Discussion

The healing of the paralytic at the Pool of Bethesda (John 5:1–15) is, on the surface, a straightforward miracle story. But once we read it with the eyes of the tradition — trained to see how the New Testament is the Old Testament unveiled — it becomes something far more: a compressed retelling of Israel's entire salvation history, and a mirror held up to the condition of every human soul in need of God's healing grace.

The Number 38: An Unmistakable Signal

John tells us that the man had been lying by the pool for thirty-eight years. This number is not chosen at random. For a reader steeped in the Old Testament, it would have resonated immediately with one of the most painful passages in the Pentateuch.

In Deuteronomy 2:14, Moses recalls the years of Israel's wandering and notes:

"And the time from our leaving Kadesh-barnea until we crossed the brook Zered was thirty-eight years, until the entire generation, the men of war, had perished from the camp, as the Lord had sworn to them."

Thirty-eight years: the precise duration of Israel's punishment in the desert, the period during which the faithless generation that refused to enter the Promised Land was consigned to paralysis, to going nowhere, to waiting for death while the next generation grew up. The paralytic has been lying by the pool for exactly this length of time. John is waving a flag for his theologically literate readers: this man is Israel.

The Pool and the Law

The Pool of Bethesda had five porticoes — and lying under those five porticoes were the multitudes of the sick, the blind, the lame, and the paralyzed, waiting for the water to be stirred. The Church Fathers, including Tertullian and later commentators, noted that five porticoes correspond to the five books of Moses — the Torah. Under the canopy of the Law, Israel lay sick and waiting.

This is a precise theological statement about the limits of the Mosaic Law. The Law, as St. Paul will later articulate with such force in his letters to the Romans and Galatians, was never designed to justify — to save — but to diagnose. It revealed the condition of sin but could not heal it. The paralytic lies under five porticoes, waiting. The Law surrounds him. But the Law cannot make him rise.

Helplessness and the Need for a Savior

The most piercing moment of this story is the paralytic's answer to Jesus's question: "Do you want to be healed?" (John 5:6). We might expect a simple yes. Instead, we get a lament:

"Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up, and while I am going another steps down before me." (John 5:7)

He has no one. Thirty-eight years, and no one has helped him. He is utterly dependent on others and utterly abandoned by them. This is Israel in Egypt — enslaved, powerless, unable to save themselves, with no deliverer in sight. It is Israel in the desert — unable to enter the land, incapable of claiming what God had promised. And in a deeper sense, it is every human soul in the condition of sin: paralyzed, unable to rise by its own power, perpetually displaced by those more agile or more favored, waiting for a healing that never comes.

This helplessness is not incidental. It is the very condition that makes the miracle possible — and that makes its meaning legible. Jesus does not come to those who have managed to limp to the pool on their own. He comes to the one who has been lying there the longest, who has the least hope, who has no one.

"Rise, Take Up Your Mat, and Walk"

Jesus does not ask for effort or merit. He commands. "Rise, take up your bed, and walk" (John 5:8). Three imperatives. And the man rises.

This is the new Exodus. Moses stretched out his staff; the sea parted. Jesus speaks, and a man paralyzed for thirty-eight years stands upright and walks. He is, in his person, the crossing of the Jordan — the end of the long paralysis, the entry into the land that could not be achieved under the Law. What the five porticoes could not accomplish, a single word from the Son of God accomplishes instantly.

The Catechism, in its treatment of the Sacrament of Penance, notes that the healings of Jesus in the Gospels are signs of the forgiveness and restoration that He confers through the sacraments of the Church (CCC 1503–1504). The study guide itself draws the explicit connection: the healing of the paralytic points to the Sacrament of Reconciliation. This is not an imposition but a recognition. Jesus's final word to the man — "Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you" (John 5:14) — makes the connection between the physical healing and the interior healing of the soul undeniable.

The Man's Response: A Warning

We cannot idealize the paralytic's response. When the Jewish authorities confront him about carrying his mat on the Sabbath, he deflects responsibility: "The man who healed me told me to" (John 5:11). And then, upon learning that his healer is Jesus, he goes and reports this to those who are seeking to persecute Him (John 5:15).

This, too, mirrors Israel's history — the pattern of receiving grace and failing to respond with corresponding gratitude and fidelity. The healed man does not seek out his healer to give thanks. He does not follow Him. He does not become a disciple. And in this he stands as a warning to all of us who receive the grace of the sacraments and then drift back into old habits, old paralysis, old indifference.

Application for the Christian Life

The man's story is our story. There are areas of every Christian life that have been paralyzed for years — perhaps decades. Sins we have confessed and returned to. Wounds we have carried so long we have come to regard them as permanent. Interior attitudes that have kept us lying by the same pool, watching others receive what we cannot seem to reach.

Jesus asks us the same question He asked the paralytic: "Do you want to be healed?" It is a more searching question than it appears. Sometimes our paralysis has become our identity. We are not sure who we would be without it. The healing Jesus offers requires that we rise — that we actually pick up our mat and walk, leaving behind the place where we have been lying for so long.

The Sacrament of Reconciliation is the privileged place where this encounter happens. The priest, acting in persona Christi, speaks the words of absolution — and Christ speaks through them as surely as He spoke to the paralytic by the pool. The thirty-eight years of waiting can end in a single moment of grace.

But the grace demands a response. "Sin no more." To receive the healing of Reconciliation and return to the same sin is to act as the paralytic did — to take the gift and walk away from the Giver. The Christian life is not a series of returns to the pool but a sustained walking: the daily choosing, in cooperation with grace, to remain upright, to remain free, to remain in the company of the One who first commanded us to rise.
