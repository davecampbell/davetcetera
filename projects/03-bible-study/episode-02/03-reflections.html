Episode 2 — Reflection Questions Discussion

Seven Signs, Seven Sacraments
Scripture Focus: John 1–2

Question 1

Can you name all seven "signs" in John's Gospel? What sacrament(s) do you think each one points to?

Discussion

One of the most luminous contributions Dr. Bergsma makes in this course is the recovery of a patristic insight that the modern Church is only now rediscovering in full: that the seven miraculous signs John records in his Gospel are not simply demonstrations of divine power, but sacramental signposts — each one pointing toward one or more of the Seven Sacraments of the Catholic Church. John himself tells us that his selection is deliberate and purposeful: "Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of his disciples that are not written in this book. But these are written so that you may come to believe" (John 20:30–31). He chose seven. The number is not accidental.

What follows is a synthesis of the seven signs with their sacramental correspondences, drawing on the logic of John's Gospel and the tradition of Catholic biblical interpretation.

Sign 1 — The Wedding at Cana: Water into Wine (John 2:1–11)
Sacrament: The Eucharist (and Matrimony)

The first sign establishes the pattern for all that follow. Jesus transforms the water of the old purification rites — water used for Jewish ceremonial washings — into an abundance of the finest wine. The quantity is staggering: six stone jars holding twenty to thirty gallons each. This is not a modest top-up of the refreshments. It is an overwhelming superabundance of the new, poured out in place of the old.

The Eucharistic connection is primary: Jesus, the true Bridegroom, provides the good wine that the human bridegroom could not. At the Last Supper, He will take the cup and declare it His Blood — the cup of the New Covenant, the "good wine kept until now" (John 2:10) in its ultimate fulfillment. The scene at Cana is the first act of what the Upper Room completes.

The setting — a wedding — also gives this sign a secondary Matrimonial dimension. Jesus sanctifies and elevates the marriage covenant by His presence and by His first act of glory. The Catechism teaches that Christ's presence at Cana confirms the goodness of marriage and inaugurates the wine of the messianic age (CCC 1613).

Sign 2 — Healing of the Royal Official's Son (John 4:46–54)
Sacrament: Anointing of the Sick

The official comes to Jesus in anguish — his son is near death. Jesus heals the boy at a distance, without touching him, through a word spoken in response to the father's desperate faith. "Go; your son will live" (John 4:50). The man believes the word and departs, and the healing is confirmed.

This sign points to the Sacrament of Anointing of the Sick, which the Catechism describes as the sacrament by which "the Church entrusts the sick to the suffering and glorified Lord" (CCC 1499). Like the official, the Church brings the sick to Christ with faith, not with sight, trusting in the power of His word to heal body and soul. The distance between Jesus and the dying child in John 4 mirrors the mystery of sacramental grace — it operates not by physical proximity but by the power of Christ's word mediated through the Church.

Sign 3 — Healing of the Paralytic at Bethesda (John 5:1–15)
Sacrament: Penance and Reconciliation

The paralytic has lain helpless for thirty-eight years. Jesus heals him with a command — "Rise, take up your mat and walk" (John 5:8) — and then, when He finds the man later in the Temple, speaks words that reveal the deeper dimension of what has happened: "See, you are well! Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you" (John 5:14).

The connection to the Sacrament of Reconciliation could not be more explicit. The healing is not merely physical; it is bound up with the forgiveness of sin. Jesus is presented here as the one with authority to release both physical and spiritual paralysis. In the Sacrament of Penance, the priest acts in persona Christi, speaking the same liberating word to souls paralyzed by sin: you are forgiven, rise and walk. Sin no more.

Sign 4 — The Feeding of the Five Thousand (John 6:1–15)
Sacrament: The Eucharist

The fourth sign is the most explicitly Eucharistic of all, and John makes certain we cannot miss it. Jesus takes bread, gives thanks (εὐχαριστήσας, the very word from which "Eucharist" derives), and distributes it to the multitude. He then delivers the Bread of Life Discourse (John 6:22–71), in which He states with unmistakable clarity: "my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink" (John 6:55). Many disciples depart at this teaching because it is too hard. Jesus does not soften it.

The feeding on the hillside is a preview and a preparation for the Eucharistic institution at the Last Supper. Both the bread distributed in John 6 and the Eucharistic loaf share the same fourfold action that marks every Eucharist: Jesus takes the bread, blesses it, breaks it, and gives it. Where the Cana miracle points to the Eucharistic cup, the Feeding of the Five Thousand points to the Eucharistic bread.

Sign 5 — Walking on Water (John 6:16–21)
Sacrament: Baptism (and Confirmation)

The disciples are caught in a storm at night, far from shore, straining at the oars. Jesus comes to them, walking on the water — across the very waters of chaos and darkness that, in the Old Testament imagination, represent the forces hostile to God and to human life. His words of self-identification — "It is I" (ἐγώ εἰμι, "I AM") — are the divine name revealed to Moses at the burning bush.

This sign points toward Baptism: Jesus as the master of the primordial waters, the One who crosses the sea of death and emerges on the other side. The Christian who is baptized enters those same waters and emerges with Christ — passing through death and darkness into new life. The crossing of the sea is one of the great Baptismal types of the Old Testament (1 Corinthians 10:1–2), and Jesus recapitulates and fulfills it. Some also see here a pointer to Confirmation — the strengthening of the disciples against fear and storm by the presence of the Spirit-filled Christ.

Sign 6 — Healing of the Man Born Blind (John 9:1–41)
Sacrament: Baptism

The sixth sign is among the richest baptismal passages in all of Scripture. A man who has been blind from birth — who has never seen the light — is healed when Jesus makes clay, anoints his eyes, and sends him to wash in the Pool of Siloam. The man washes and comes back seeing.

Every element of this sign resonates with Baptismal theology. The clay made from earth and spittle echoes Genesis 2:7 — God forming man from the dust of the ground. The anointing of the eyes prefigures the anointing with chrism at Baptism. The washing in the Pool of Siloam — whose name John translates as "Sent" (ἀπεσταλμένος), pointing to the one who is sent from the Father — is the sacramental bath that opens the eyes to divine light. It is for good reason that the early Church called Baptism photismos — enlightenment, illumination. The man born blind is the paradigmatic image of what Baptism accomplishes: to be plunged into the waters and to emerge seeing for the first time.

Sign 7 — The Raising of Lazarus (John 11:1–44)
Sacrament: Baptism (and Anointing of the Sick)

The seventh and greatest sign is the raising of Lazarus, which functions as the culminating preview of the Resurrection itself. Jesus stands before the sealed tomb — "Lazarus, come out!" (John 11:43) — and a man four days dead walks out into the light, still bound in his burial cloths.

The primary sacramental resonance is with Baptism: the passage from death to life, the emergence from the tomb-womb of the waters into the light of the risen Christ. In the early Church, baptisteries were often constructed in octagonal shapes symbolizing the eighth day — the day beyond the seven-day week, the day of the Resurrection, the day Lazarus prefigures. To be baptized is to be Lazarus — called out of the tomb by the voice of the Lord, unbound, and sent into the community of the living.

The secondary resonance with Anointing of the Sick is also real: the sacrament the Church has always associated with the dying and the dead, the anointing that prepares the soul for its passage from this world to the next. Jesus delays His coming to Lazarus by two days — long enough to ensure the man is unmistakably dead — so that the glory of God might be fully revealed. The sacrament does not always restore physical life. But it always opens onto the eternal life that Lazarus's resurrection signifies.

Question 2

The Prologue of John states that "the darkness has not overcome [the light]" (John 1:5). What do you think John means by this? What might he be trying to tell us about Jesus' life and ministry as a whole?

Discussion

John 1:5 is a thesis statement for the entire Gospel. Every episode that follows — every sign, every controversy, every moment of misunderstanding and rejection, and ultimately the Passion itself — must be read in its light. To understand this verse is to hold the interpretive key to the whole of John's theological vision.

The Double Meaning: A Deliberate Ambiguity

The Greek verb that most English translations render as "overcome" is κατέλαβεν (katelaben), from the root καταλαμβάνω (katalambanō). It is a word with a rich semantic range, and John almost certainly intends both of its primary meanings simultaneously.

On the one hand, katalambanō means to overtake, seize, or overpower. On the other hand, it means to comprehend, understand, or grasp. Both meanings are operative in John 1:5, and this is not accidental. John is the most theologically self-conscious author in the New Testament, and his use of polyvalent language — words and images that carry multiple meanings at once — is one of the defining characteristics of his literary style.

Read in the first sense, verse 5 is a cosmic declaration: the darkness has not overpowered the light. The forces of evil, sin, and death — what John elsewhere calls the ruler of this world (John 12:31; 14:30) — have not been able to extinguish the divine light that entered the world in the person of Jesus Christ.

Read in the second sense, verse 5 is a statement about incomprehension: the world did not understand the light. John 1:10–11 develops this second meaning explicitly: "He was in the world, and the world came to be through him, but the world did not know him. He came to what was his own, but his own people did not accept him." The darkness cannot even comprehend what it is up against.

John holds both meanings together because they interpret each other. The world's failure to understand Jesus is itself a form of its failure to overcome Him. The powers of darkness think they are defeating the light when they send Jesus to the Cross. They cannot understand what they are looking at. And precisely in that incomprehension, the light triumphs.

The Cross as the Apparent Victory of Darkness

This verse functions as a proleptic anticipation of the Passion narrative. If you read John's account of the arrest, trial, and crucifixion of Jesus without the lens of verse 1:5, it can appear to be a story of darkness winning. The authorities plot, Judas betrays, the crowd shouts for crucifixion, the soldiers nail the light of the world to a cross at a place called the Skull. The darkness seems to have seized its prey at last.

But John presents the Passion in an entirely different key. In his Gospel, the Cross is not the defeat of Jesus — it is His exaltation. He speaks of being "lifted up" (John 3:14; 8:28; 12:32) using a verb that means both physical elevation and glorification. The "hour" that Jesus keeps deferring throughout the Gospel — "My hour has not yet come" (John 2:4) — turns out to be the hour of His death, which is simultaneously the hour of His glory (John 12:23: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified").

Darkness rushes upon the light and finds that it cannot hold it. The tomb is sealed. Soldiers stand guard. And on the third day, the stone is rolled back and the light of the Resurrection blazes out — not despite the darkness that tried to contain it, but through it.

The Ongoing Tension

It would be a mistake to read John 1:5 as a statement about a battle already over. The tension between light and darkness runs through the entire Gospel and, the Church teaches, through the entire history of the world until Christ returns. Every soul is, in some sense, Nicodemus coming to Jesus at night — aware of the light, drawn toward it, but not yet fully emerged from the darkness.

John's declaration that the darkness has not overcome the light is not a smugly triumphalist claim. It is a promise, spoken at the threshold of the Gospel, to sustain the reader through everything that follows: the misunderstandings, the rejections, the betrayals, the suffering, and the silence of the tomb. The light has not been put out. It never will be.

Question 3

In John 2:21, why does Jesus speak of "the temple of his body"? By using this phrase, what is He revealing about His identity?

Discussion

The Cleansing of the Temple in John 2:13–22 is one of the most theologically charged scenes in the entire Gospel, and the verse at its center — "he was speaking about the temple of his body" (John 2:21) — is among the most direct claims to divine identity in all of Scripture.

The Temple and the Presence of God

To grasp what Jesus is claiming when He identifies His body as the Temple, we must first understand what the Temple was. It was not merely a religious building or a site of ritual observance. It was the dwelling place of the divine Presence — the one location on earth where heaven and earth intersected, where God's glory (kavod, Shekinah) was concretely, tangibly present among His people.

The history of God's presence among Israel follows a trajectory: the Tabernacle in the wilderness, where the glory-cloud descended and filled the tent (Exodus 40:34); the Temple of Solomon, into which the glory of the Lord entered at its dedication so powerfully that the priests could not stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10–11); the exile, during which Ezekiel saw the glory depart from the Temple (Ezekiel 10–11); and the return from exile, after which the Second Temple stood — magnificent in its reconstruction under Herod, but conspicuously, achingly, without the divine Shekinah. The glory had not returned.

Into this context of absence and longing, John announces in his Prologue: "The Word became flesh and dwelt [tabernacled] among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). The glory has returned. It has not returned to the stone Temple on the hill. It has come to dwell in a human body — the body of the eternal Son.

The Claim of John 2:21

When Jesus says "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19), His hearers understand Him to be making a grandiose and offensive claim about the Jerusalem Temple — the largest and most magnificent building most of them had ever seen, forty-six years in the building. Their incomprehension is understandable.

But John, with his characteristic technique of dramatic irony, tells us what Jesus actually meant: He was speaking about the temple of his body (John 2:21). This is one of the most compressed and explosive theological statements in the New Testament. Unpacked, it contains at least four distinct claims about Jesus's identity:

First, Jesus is identifying Himself as the true dwelling place of God — the fulfillment of everything the Temple was meant to be. He does not come to worship in the Temple as one among many. He comes as the One to whom the Temple pointed and in whose presence it becomes obsolete. As the Catechism states: "Jesus acted as the One who is master of the Temple, which points to His divine identity" (CCC 586).

Second, Jesus is claiming sovereignty over His own death and resurrection. "In three days I will raise it up" — not it will be raised, but I will raise it. He is not speaking as a victim awaiting an execution. He is speaking as the One who lays down His life and takes it up again by His own authority (John 10:18). This is a claim only God could make.

Third, the identification of His body as the Temple is a key to understanding the theology of the Eucharist and the Church. If Jesus is the Temple — the place where God dwells — then where His body is present, there is the divine Presence. The Eucharist, in which His Body and Blood are truly present, makes every church building in the Christian world a site of the divine Shekinah in a way the Jerusalem Temple never was. And the body of each believer, incorporated into Christ through Baptism and nourished by the Eucharist, becomes itself a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19).

Fourth, the phrase "in three days" points unmistakably forward to the Resurrection. John tells us that only after the Resurrection did the disciples understand what Jesus had meant (John 2:22). The entire exchange at the Temple is written from the vantage point of Easter morning — from the perspective of those who have seen the destroyed temple of His body raised on the third day and who now understand, with the luminous clarity of retrospection, the full weight of what He had said.

The New Temple Theology of John

This passage is the fulcrum on which John's entire Temple theology turns. John 4:21–24 — Jesus telling the Samaritan woman that the hour is coming when worship will be "in Spirit and truth", neither on Mount Gerizim nor in Jerusalem — continues the same logic. The Jerusalem Temple is being superseded not because it was wrong, but because it was a sign pointing toward what has now arrived. The sign yields to the reality it signified. In Jesus, the glory has returned — and it has come to stay.
