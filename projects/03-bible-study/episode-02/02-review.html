Seven Signs, Seven Sacraments
Scripture Focus: John 1–2

Question 3

True or False: Jesus is the new Isaac and the new Moses.

  • a. True
  • b. False

Answer: FALSE

Discussion

This question is an invitation to precision — and precision in biblical typology matters enormously, because misidentifying the type obscures the theological truth the sacred author is actually communicating. In Episode 2, Dr. Bergsma draws our attention to the specific typological identities that John's Gospel is constructing in chapters 1 and 2. They are not Moses and Isaac. They are Adam and the Bridegroom.

Jesus as the New Adam

The signal is sounded in the very first words of the Gospel: "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1). This is not accidental phrasing. The opening words of John's Prologue — ἐν ἀρχῇ (en archē), "in the beginning" — are the precise words that open the Septuagint's translation of Genesis: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). John is announcing, before a single episode of Jesus's ministry has been narrated, that what we are about to read is a new Genesis. The story of Jesus is the story of a new creation.

Where the first Adam was formed from the dust of the old creation, the new Adam — the Word made flesh — enters the world from eternity itself. Where the first Adam failed in the garden, succumbing to the serpent and plunging all creation into darkness, the new Adam will succeed. He is, as St. Paul will later articulate in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15, the last Adam — the one who undoes what the first Adam did and recapitulates all of human history in Himself.

John reinforces this Adam typology throughout chapters 1 and 2. The seven-day structure that Dr. Bergsma identifies in John 1:1–2:11 — in which the narrative unfolds across a series of successive days — is designed to evoke the seven days of creation in Genesis 1. Jesus does not simply teach about a new creation; He embodies and inaugurates it.

Jesus as the Eschatological Bridegroom

Alongside the Adam typology runs the Bridegroom typology, which reaches its first climax at the Wedding of Cana. The scene is immediately recognizable to any reader formed in the Old Testament as a betrothal type-scene. In the patriarchal narratives, a man journeys to a foreign land, meets a woman, and a marriage covenant follows. Isaac's servant meets Rebekah at a well. Jacob meets Rachel at a well. Moses meets Zipporah at a well. The pattern is so consistent that a first-century Jewish reader would have understood immediately what John was doing.

When Jesus arrives at a wedding in Cana and provides the wine that the bridegroom failed to supply, He is assuming the role of the true Bridegroom — the one the prophets had promised would come to claim His bride. Mary's role in this scene further establishes the typology: she functions as the new Eve, the mother of the new Adam, interceding on behalf of those in need. Her words to the servants — "Do whatever he tells you" (John 2:5) — are a deliberate echo of the obedience that Eve failed to render.

Why Not Moses and Isaac?

The "new Moses" typology is real and deeply present in the New Testament, but it is primarily a Matthean construction. Matthew structures his Gospel around five great discourses that parallel the five books of the Torah, presenting Jesus as the new lawgiver ascending a new Sinai. John is doing something different. His Gospel is oriented not toward Sinai but toward Eden and the Wedding Feast — not toward the giving of the Law but toward the restoration of creation and the fulfillment of the spousal covenant.

The "new Isaac" typology — centered on the Aqedah, the Binding of Isaac in Genesis 22 — is theologically profound and indeed points to the Passion. But it is not the operative typology of John 1–2. Bergsma is asking us, in this question, to read attentively — to notice which Old Testament figures John is actually invoking, rather than importing frameworks from other parts of the New Testament canon.

The answer is False: in the context of Episode 2 and John's Gospel as a whole, Jesus is presented as the new Adam and the eschatological Bridegroom — not primarily as the new Isaac or the new Moses.

Question 4

True or False: The Prologue of John's Gospel is closely tied to the theme of creation found in the opening chapter of Exodus.

  • a. True
  • b. False

Answer: FALSE

Discussion

This question tests something deceptively simple: attentiveness to which book of the Old Testament the Prologue is actually echoing. The answer is not Exodus. It is Genesis.

"In the Beginning" — The Echo of Genesis

The entire interpretive logic of the Prologue depends on recognizing its opening words as a deliberate citation of the very first verse of the Bible. John 1:1 — "In the beginning was the Word" — does not open a discussion about the Exodus, the Red Sea, the giving of the Law at Sinai, or any of the great themes of the second book of Moses. It opens with the identical phrase that opens Genesis 1:1: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."

In Hebrew, this is bereshit: the first word of the entire Torah, and the Hebrew title of the book of Genesis itself. In the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament used by the early Church, Genesis 1:1 opens with ἐν ἀρχῇ — exactly the words John chooses. This is not coincidence. It is a literary and theological declaration: what you are about to read begins where everything began.

The Seven-Day Structure

The connection to Genesis 1 goes far deeper than a shared opening phrase. Dr. Bergsma draws our attention to the seven-day structure embedded in John 1:1–2:11. The narrative of John's first two chapters, carefully analyzed, unfolds across a series of days that culminate in the Wedding at Cana on "the third day" — and the total count, when the days are traced precisely, reaches seven. John is presenting the ministry of Jesus as a new week of creation, culminating in the gift of the good wine at a wedding feast — a new Sabbath joy, a new rest, a new Eden.

This structural parallel to Genesis 1's seven days of creation is central to John's theological purpose. Jesus does not merely interpret or supplement the old creation. He is the agent of a new one. "All things came into being through him, and without him not one thing came into being" (John 1:3) — this is not a statement about Moses or the Exodus. It is a statement about the eternal Word who was present at the first creation and who now enters that creation to renew it from within.

Why the Confusion Might Arise

It is worth noting why Exodus might seem like a plausible answer. The theme of divine glory (doxa in Greek; kavod in Hebrew) — which John invokes in the Prologue ("we have seen his glory", John 1:14) and at Cana ("he revealed his glory", John 2:11) — is indeed associated with the Exodus narrative. The divine glory filled the Tabernacle in Exodus 40:34-35. The Shekinah cloud accompanied Israel through the wilderness. John is certainly aware of this Exodus resonance, and he will draw on it throughout his Gospel.

But the foundational Old Testament text for the Prologue is not Exodus 1. It is Genesis 1. The creation of light, the ordering of chaos, the breath of God hovering over the waters — these are the images that underlie John's presentation of the eternal Word. When John writes that "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), the Greek verb ἐσκήνωσεν (eskēnōsen) — literally "pitched his tent" or "tabernacled" — does echo the Exodus Tabernacle. But this is a secondary resonance enriching an already established Genesis framework, not the primary text being cited.

The answer is False: the Prologue of John's Gospel is closely tied to the theme of creation found in the opening chapter of Genesis, not Exodus.
